What are Power Amplifiers?

A Power Amplifier (Large Signal Amplifier) is designed to deliver maximum power to a load (like a speaker or antenna). They operate over a large portion of the transistor's characteristic curve.

Class A Amplifier

The transistor conducts for the full 360° of the input cycle. The Q-point is exactly in the center of the load line.

Fig 2: Class A Operation & Waveform
  • Efficiency: Max 25% (Direct) / 50% (Transformer Coupled).
  • Distortion: Lowest among all classes.
  • Application: High-fidelity audio pre-amps.

Class B Amplifier

The transistor conducts for only 180° (half cycle). Usually used in a Push-Pull configuration where one transistor handles the positive half and the other handles the negative.

Fig 3: Class B Push-Pull Circuit

Crossover Distortion: Occurs because transistors don't turn on until the signal reaches ~0.7V.

Fig 4: Crossover Distortion Visualized

Class AB Amplifier

A compromise between A and B. It conducts for slightly more than 180° to eliminate crossover distortion.

Fig 5: Class AB Biasing

This is the most common class used in high-quality audio power amplifiers today.

Class C Amplifier

The transistor conducts for less than 180°. It produces high distortion but very high efficiency (over 80%).

Fig 6: Class C with LC Tank Circuit

Note: Used almost exclusively in Radio Frequency (RF) transmitters where the "Tank Circuit" filters out the distortion.
